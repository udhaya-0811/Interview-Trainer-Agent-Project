## S1 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** What is effective communication in a professional setting?

**Answer:**
Effective professional communication has several dimensions:
- **Clarity:** use simple, precise language; avoid jargon unless your audience uses it.
- **Conciseness:** respect the listener's time; lead with the key point (inverted pyramid).
- **Active listening:** listen to understand, not to respond; summarise what you heard before replying.
- **Adaptability:** adjust style for audience (technical depth for engineers, business terms for executives).
- **Written communication:** asynchronous messages should be complete (context + ask + deadline) to avoid back-and-forth.
- **Proactive communication:** surface blockers, delays, and risks before they escalate; don't wait to be asked.
In software teams, communication includes code reviews, design documents, PR descriptions, and status updates — all require the same intentionality.

## S2 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you approach giving and receiving feedback?

**Answer:**
**Giving feedback:**
- Use the SBI (Situation-Behaviour-Impact) model: describe the specific situation, the observable behaviour, and its impact (not character judgements).
- Focus on actions and outcomes, not personality.
- Be timely (close to the event) and private (unless public recognition).
- Make it a two-way conversation: "This is my perception — what was your intent?"

**Receiving feedback:**
- Assume positive intent unless proven otherwise.
- Listen fully before responding; resist the urge to defend immediately.
- Ask clarifying questions to understand specifically what needs to change.
- Thank the person for the feedback, then process and decide what to act on.
Psychological safety: teams where feedback is normalised (regular 1:1s, blameless retrospectives) outperform those where it accumulates into annual surprise reviews.

## S3 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you manage your time and stay productive?

**Answer:**
Productivity strategies for knowledge workers:
1. **Time blocking:** schedule deep work (coding, writing) in uninterrupted 90-minute blocks; batch shallow work (email, meetings) separately.
2. **Task management:** capture everything in a trusted system (Jira, Notion, Todoist). Daily prioritisation to know the top 3 outcomes for the day.
3. **Weekly review:** 30-minute review every Friday — what's done, what's carrying over, what's upcoming.
4. **Context switching reduction:** finish one task before starting another; use "parking lot" notes for distracting thoughts.
5. **Energy management:** do cognitively demanding work during peak energy hours; save routine tasks for low-energy periods.
6. **Say no effectively:** "I want to help with this; can we discuss the timeline given I have X in progress?"

## S4 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** What is emotional intelligence (EQ) and why does it matter at work?

**Answer:**
Emotional intelligence is the ability to recognise, understand, manage, and use emotions effectively in yourself and others.
Five components (Goleman):
1. **Self-awareness:** recognise your own emotions and their impact.
2. **Self-regulation:** manage disruptive emotions; think before acting.
3. **Motivation:** intrinsically driven to achieve.
4. **Empathy:** understand others' perspectives and emotions.
5. **Social skills:** build relationships, navigate social situations effectively.
At work, high EQ predicts leadership effectiveness, team collaboration, and resilience to setbacks — often more than technical IQ. In software: EQ is critical for navigating design disagreements, supporting struggling team members, and communicating technical risks to non-technical stakeholders.

## S5 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you approach conflict resolution in a team?

**Answer:**
Conflict resolution framework:
1. **Separate people from problems:** focus on the issue, not the person. "How do we solve this?" not "You caused this."
2. **Understand all perspectives:** actively listen to each side; seek to understand before seeking to be understood.
3. **Identify interests, not positions:** the stated position ("I want X") often masks the underlying interest ("I need X because I'm worried about Y").
4. **Generate options:** brainstorm solutions together before evaluating any.
5. **Agree on objective criteria:** resolve factual disagreements with data, benchmarks, or expert input.
6. **Escalate when necessary:** if direct resolution fails, involve a neutral party (manager, tech lead).
In technical teams: most conflicts are about trade-offs (speed vs quality, simplicity vs flexibility). Data and prototypes resolve these faster than debate.

## S6 | difficulty: advanced
**Question:** What is leadership and how do you show it without a formal title?

**Answer:**
Leadership is the ability to influence outcomes and others' development, irrespective of positional authority.
Ways to lead without a title:
- **Technical leadership:** set standards through example (code quality, documentation, testing), author ADRs (Architecture Decision Records), mentor others.
- **Process leadership:** identify friction and propose improvements; own follow-through.
- **Cultural leadership:** model the behaviour you want to see (transparency, direct feedback, blameless retrospectives), celebrate others' successes.
- **Stakeholder communication:** translate technical complexity for business audiences, building trust with cross-functional partners.
- **Initiative:** identify problems nobody owns and take responsibility for moving them forward.
The "lead from any seat" principle: seniority is about scope of impact and judgment, not just tenure or title.

## S7 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you build rapport and trust with colleagues?

**Answer:**
Trust is built through consistency, competence, and care.
- **Consistency:** do what you say you'll do. Under-promise, over-deliver. If you can't deliver, communicate early.
- **Competence:** deliver quality work; share knowledge; be the person others can rely on for honest technical answers.
- **Care:** show genuine interest in colleagues — their challenges, growth, and wellbeing. Remember and follow up on things they mention.
- **Vulnerability:** admitting "I don't know" or "I was wrong" builds trust faster than projecting infallibility.
- **Active listening:** giving full attention (not multitasking) is one of the most powerful rapport-building acts.
In remote teams: proactively over-communicate; reach out informally (virtual coffee, non-work channels); be a responsive async communicator.

## S8 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you adapt to change and uncertainty at work?

**Answer:**
Adaptability in a professional context:
- **Reframe change as information:** a pivot or reprioritisation means the organisation has new data; curiosity ("why?") replaces resistance.
- **Focus on what you can control:** your response, preparation, and quality of work within the new context.
- **Communicate impact:** if a change significantly affects your work, escalate the trade-offs clearly and early so stakeholders can decide with full information.
- **Build range:** deliberately learn adjacent skills (new languages, tools, domains) so you're not brittle to technological change.
- **Resilience is a skill:** debrief after setbacks, identify lessons, apply them. Treat failures as data points, not verdicts.
For tech specifically: the half-life of specific frameworks is short; learning fundamentals deeply (CS, systems, communication, product thinking) creates lasting adaptability.

## S9 | difficulty: advanced
**Question:** What makes a high-performing engineering team?

**Answer:**
Research (Google's Project Aristotle) identified psychological safety as the top predictor of team effectiveness.
High-performing engineering teams share:
1. **Psychological safety:** team members feel safe to take risks, admit mistakes, and ask questions without fear of punishment.
2. **Clarity:** everyone knows team goals, their role, and how success is measured.
3. **Dependability:** team members reliably deliver on commitments.
4. **Meaning:** the work connects to something personally significant for each member.
5. **Impact:** the team believes their work matters.
Engineering-specific enablers: good CI/CD (fast feedback loops), strong code review culture, investment in developer tooling, clear escalation paths, and leaders who shield the team from unnecessary noise.
Leadership behaviour: create psychological safety, clarify direction, remove blockers, develop individuals.

## S10 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you approach mentoring junior team members?

**Answer:**
Effective mentoring principles:
1. **Listen first:** understand their goals, current challenges, and learning style before prescribing solutions.
2. **Guide, don't do:** when they're stuck, ask guiding questions ("What have you tried? What's your hypothesis?") rather than immediately giving answers. This builds their problem-solving muscle.
3. **Pair programme:** work alongside them on real tasks; narrate your thinking process — this transfers judgment and heuristics, not just knowledge.
4. **Give regular, specific feedback:** timely, actionable, positive and constructive.
5. **Celebrate growth:** acknowledge progress explicitly; junior engineers often don't see how much they've improved.
6. **Set boundaries:** you can support growth without being a 24/7 help desk. Teach them to research independently first.
7. **Remember your own journey:** empathy for their frustrations builds trust.

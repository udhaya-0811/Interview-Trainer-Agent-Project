# HR & Situational Interview Guidelines
# Category: HR | All Roles

---
## H1 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** Tell me about yourself.

**Answer:**
This is a structured professional pitch, not a life story. Use the Present → Past → Future framework:
- **Present:** current role/situation and key achievement.
- **Past:** what led you here (relevant education, experience, milestone).
- **Future:** why this role and company, and what you aim to contribute.
Keep it to 90-120 seconds. Tailor to the job description.
Example structure: "I'm a final-year Computer Science student at [University] with a focus on backend development. Over the past two years I've interned at two product companies where I built REST APIs and deployed microservices. I'm excited about this role because [specific company reason] and I want to grow in [specific technical area]."

---
## H2 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** Why do you want to work for this company?

**Answer:**
Research-based answer is essential. Mention:
1. **Specific product/technology:** "Your migration to event-driven microservices with Kafka is exactly the architecture I want to deepen expertise in."
2. **Mission/values alignment:** "Your engineering blog's emphasis on developer experience and internal platform teams aligns with how I believe software organisations should scale."
3. **Growth opportunity:** "The exposure to working on systems at [company scale] would accelerate my growth faster than similar roles at smaller companies."
Avoid: "I need a job," generic praise, or mentioning salary.

---
## H3 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** What are your greatest strengths?

**Answer:**
Select 2-3 genuine strengths relevant to the role. Each must be substantiated with a specific example.
Framework: Strength → Evidence → Relevance.
Example: "One of my strengths is systematic debugging. When I encounter a complex bug, I form hypotheses, isolate variables, and document findings — rather than randomly trying fixes. This helped me resolve a subtle race condition in a multithreaded service that had evaded the team for two weeks. For this role, where your systems serve millions of users, that methodical approach to production issues would be directly useful."
Avoid: "I'm a hard worker," "I'm a perfectionist" (cliché), or strengths unrelated to the role.

---
## H4 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** What are your greatest weaknesses?

**Answer:**
Choose a genuine weakness that is not a core job requirement. Show self-awareness and active improvement.
Framework: Weakness → Awareness → Mitigation in progress → Progress made.
Example: "Public speaking used to be a significant challenge for me. I avoided presenting in team meetings and deferred to others. I recognised this would limit my career growth, so I joined my university's debate club and have since voluntarily presented at two internal tech talks. I'm still improving, but I'm no longer avoiding these opportunities."
Avoid: "I work too hard," faking a strength as a weakness, or core-role weaknesses (saying you're bad at coding for a software role).

---
## H5 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** Where do you see yourself in 5 years?

**Answer:**
Answer shows ambition aligned with realistic growth within the company's context.
Framework: Technical growth → Leadership aspiration → Alignment with company direction.
"In five years, I aim to be a senior engineer who is a recognised technical expert in distributed systems, having led at least one significant architectural initiative. I'd like to begin taking on mentoring responsibilities for junior engineers. I'm attracted to this company because the engineering organisation seems to have clear paths to both the individual contributor and management tracks, and I'd want to evaluate both after getting deeper into the technical work."
Avoid: "I want your manager's job," vague "grow as a professional," or stating a role at a different company.

---
## H6 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** Why are you leaving your current/previous job?

**Answer:**
Keep it professional. Focus on positive motivations (growth, challenge, alignment) rather than negative criticism of past employer.
Good reasons: "I've learned a lot in my current role but feel I've reached a ceiling for growth in this specific area," "I'm looking for a company whose scale will expose me to the kind of technical challenges I want to solve," "The team structure at this company gives me the opportunity to take on more ownership."
Avoid: badmouthing colleagues/management, focusing on salary as the primary reason (even if true), vague "time for a change."

---
## H7 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you handle stress and pressure?

**Answer:**
Show that you have concrete, healthy coping strategies and can maintain performance under pressure.
Framework: Acknowledge pressure is real → concrete strategy → example of it working.
"I distinguish between productive pressure (tight deadlines with clear goals) and unproductive stress (ambiguity, conflicting priorities). For the former, I break the work into the smallest possible tasks and focus on the next action. For ambiguity, I surface blockers early and ask clarifying questions rather than letting them accumulate. During our last release, when we had a critical bug 24 hours before launch, I stayed calm by focusing on what I could control: triaging, fixing, and communicating clearly. The bug was resolved and the release shipped on time."

## H8 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** What is your expected salary?

**Answer:**
Research-informed, range-based answer.
1. Research market rate: Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, LinkedIn salary insights for the role, location, and experience level.
2. Provide a range with your target at the lower end: "Based on my research and [experience], I'm targeting ₹X - ₹Y CTC. I'm open to discussing the full compensation package."
3. In early rounds: "I'd prefer to understand the full role requirements and compensation structure before committing to a number — can you share the band for this position?"
Avoid: giving a number too early, saying "whatever you offer," or providing a range you're not comfortable accepting at the low end.

## H9 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** Do you prefer working independently or in a team?

**Answer:**
Most roles require both; demonstrate versatility.
"I genuinely enjoy both, and I've found that the most effective engineers can do deep focused work independently and also collaborate fluidly. I do my best creative/technical work in focused solo sessions — architecture design, complex debugging. But I value team settings for code reviews, design discussions, and knowledge sharing, because I've consistently found that my solutions improve when reviewed by others. In practice, I aim for a balance where I come to team interactions prepared with a concrete proposal, which makes those sessions more productive."

---
## H10 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** What motivates you?

**Answer:**
Authentic answer connecting intrinsic motivation to the nature of the work.
Framework: Core motivator → Why it matters to you → How it shows in your work.
"I'm primarily motivated by solving problems that genuinely matter to end users and seeing the direct impact of my work. When I see that code I wrote is used by thousands of people, or that a process improvement saved my team hours per week, that's deeply satisfying. I'm also motivated by continuous learning — I get real energy from mastering new technologies and teaching what I've learned to others. That combination drives me toward companies building products at scale with an engineering culture that values craft and learning."

## H11 | difficulty: advanced
**Question:** How would you approach the first 30/60/90 days in this role?

**Answer:**
Shows planning, thoughtfulness, and initiative.
- **First 30 days (Learn):** deep onboarding. Read the codebase, understand the architecture, attend all team rituals, pair with each team member, understand the product from a user perspective. Resist the urge to "fix" things immediately.
- **First 60 days (Contribute):** take ownership of one or two small but meaningful tasks. Deliver them with high quality. Ask questions constantly but also bring proposed solutions. Understand what success looks like for this role.
- **First 90 days (Drive):** have a clear picture of the team's priorities and pain points. Propose one improvement (process, technical, or product) based on what you've learned. Build the relationships needed to get things done.

## H12 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** Do you have any questions for us?

**Answer:**
Always have 3-5 thoughtful questions prepared. Categories:
1. **Team & culture:** "What does a typical day look like for engineers on this team?" "How does the team handle technical disagreements?"
2. **Role & growth:** "What does success look like in this role after 6 months?" "What are the biggest technical challenges the team is working through right now?"
3. **Company/product:** "Where do you see the product in the next 12 months?" "What is the biggest risk the company faces?"
4. **Your interviewers:** "What do you enjoy most about working here?"
Avoid: salary/benefits (unless asked), questions answered on the website, questions that imply you weren't listening.

## H13 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** How do you prioritise your work when everything feels urgent?

**Answer:**
Show systematic thinking and communication skills.
Framework: Impact assessment → Stakeholder alignment → Execution → Communication.
"I first distinguish between urgency (time-sensitive) and importance (high impact). I use a 2×2 impact/urgency matrix to triage. I then validate my prioritisation by quickly checking with my manager or key stakeholders to ensure I'm not missing context. For items I can't address immediately, I set realistic expectations upfront and communicate a timeline. I also try to protect focus time for high-impact, deep work by batching communication (email, Slack) rather than context-switching constantly. The key principle is: urgent work should not always default to the top."

---
## H14 | difficulty: intermediate
**Question:** What do you know about our company and products?

**Answer:**
Research thoroughly before the interview:
1. **Core product and business model:** what does the company sell, to whom, and how does it make money?
2. **Recent news:** funding, product launches, partnerships, challenges.
3. **Engineering culture:** engineering blog, GitHub, tech stack, job descriptions.
4. **Competitive landscape:** who are their competitors and how do they differentiate?
Template answer: "I know you're a [B2B/B2C] company offering [product] primarily targeting [market]. I read your engineering blog post about [specific topic] and was impressed by [specific aspect]. I also noticed that you recently [relevant news] — I was curious how that might shape the technical roadmap for the team I'd be joining."

## H15 | difficulty: beginner
**Question:** Are you comfortable with remote work / hybrid work?

**Answer:**
Be honest about your preference and demonstrate that you are effective in the stated format.
Remote: "I've worked effectively remotely by over-communicating asynchronously, maintaining clear documentation, and being disciplined about my work environment and schedule. I'm comfortable with [tools: Slack, Jira, Zoom, Notion] and have learned to be explicit about progress and blockers in a way that I might not have needed in an office."
Hybrid: "I find the hybrid model offers a good balance — focused deep work at home, collaborative sessions in the office. I'm structured about scheduling office days around team events and design discussions where in-person collaboration adds the most value."
